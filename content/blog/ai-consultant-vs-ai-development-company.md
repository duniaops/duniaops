If your organisation wants to use AI, the first buying decision is often
unclear. Do you need someone to advise on the opportunity, a team to build the
software, or a partner who can do both? The answer depends less on a supplier's
job title than on the decisions and delivery work you need to own.

This guide helps you define that work before comparing an AI consultant, an AI
development company or a combined engagement.

## What does an AI consultant do?

An AI consultant may help you identify useful opportunities, assess data and
technical readiness, compare possible approaches, set priorities or define a
responsible adoption plan. Some consultants also build prototypes or deliver
production systems; others focus on strategy and recommendations. Confirm the
actual scope rather than assuming from the title.

Advisory work is particularly useful when the business problem is understood
but the right intervention is not. For example, a team may need to decide
whether a document workflow is suitable for automation, what information the
system can use, or how a person should review consequential outputs.

## What does an AI development company do?

An AI development company should be able to turn a defined need into working
software. That can involve product design, data and system integration,
application engineering, model or service selection, evaluation, deployment
and ongoing support. The delivery partner should be clear about which of these
activities it will own—and what it expects your team to provide.

Implementation is not simply connecting an API to a screen. The
[National Cyber Security Centre's secure AI guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/guidelines-secure-ai-system-development)
covers security across the system lifecycle, including design, development,
deployment and operation. The UK Government's [AI Risk Management Toolkit
guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-risk-management-toolkit/ai-risk-management-toolkit-guidance)
also frames risk management as work to consider throughout design and use,
not a one-off model choice.

## Choose by the work that is still uncertain

| Your current situation | A sensible starting point | What to agree before work begins |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You have several possible use cases but no evidence about which matters | Advisory discovery or a time-boxed feasibility phase | Questions to answer, people and data needed, and the decision the work will enable |
| The use case is clear, but data, integration or quality risks are unknown | A feasibility engagement combining product, data and engineering input | A representative test, access boundaries, success criteria and stop conditions |
| The workflow and expected result are defined and you need working software | An implementation partner with product and engineering ownership | Deliverables, integration boundary, evaluation, deployment and handover |
| You have an internal team but need specialist judgement or temporary capacity | A consultant or engineers embedded with your team | Decision rights, pairing, documentation and knowledge transfer |
| You need advice now and a production system later | A staged combination of advisory and implementation | Separate decision gates; do not pre-commit to a build before the evidence is reviewed |

These options are not mutually exclusive. A development company may provide
strong discovery, and an adviser may help deliver a prototype. What matters is
that recommendations, implementation and operational responsibility are
explicitly assigned.

## Questions to ask before choosing a partner

Use questions that expose how the work will happen, not just which models or
tools a supplier knows:

1. **What decision or workflow are we trying to improve?** Ask the partner to
   describe the users, inputs, expected result and current process in plain
   language.
2. **What evidence will show that the approach is useful?** Agree test cases
   based on representative work, including examples where the system should
   fail safely or involve a person.
3. **What data and systems are involved?** Clarify data quality, permissions,
   integrations, retention expectations and which providers will receive
   information. The [ICO's AI and data protection guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/about-this-guidance/)
   is a useful starting point for identifying data-protection questions; the
   ICO notes that this guidance is under review following the Data (Use and
   Access) Act changes. It is not a substitute for advice on your particular
   obligations.
4. **Who is accountable after launch?** Ask who monitors the feature, handles
   incidents, reviews changes and decides when a human should take over.
5. **What will we own at handover?** Confirm access to code, configuration,
   evaluation material, documentation and the accounts needed to operate the
   service.

Be wary of a proposal that promises accuracy, savings or a delivery date
before it understands the workflow, data and acceptance criteria. A credible
partner should make assumptions visible and explain how uncertainty will be
reduced.

## A staged engagement keeps the decision reversible

When important details are unknown, separate the work into a decision phase
and a delivery phase. Start by mapping the workflow, data, users, risks and
dependencies. Then test the smallest useful path against agreed examples. If
the evidence supports proceeding, define production engineering, controls,
ownership and support as part of the build—not as optional work after a demo.

## The right choice is the one that leaves clear ownership

Choose advice when the main need is to make a better decision. Choose an
implementation partner when the work is to deliver and operate software. Use
both when the project needs a staged path from uncertainty to production. In
every case, agree what evidence will guide the next step and who remains
responsible when the engagement ends.
