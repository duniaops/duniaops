**One Moon distance is about 384,400 kilometres.** It is the average distance from Earth to the Moon, and it gives a manageable yardstick for much larger space numbers. NASA/JPL uses the same fixed value for the unit **LD** (lunar distance). The real Moon moves closer and farther as it orbits; one LD remains 384,400 km for comparisons.

## Read half, one and two Moon distances

Imagine a paper line with Earth at **0 cm** and one Moon distance at **10 cm**:

- **0.5 LD:** about **192,200 km** from Earth; mark **5 cm**.
- **1 LD:** about **384,400 km**; mark **10 cm**.
- **2 LD:** about **768,800 km**; mark **20 cm**.

These are **made-up visitor examples**, not today's asteroid predictions. A smaller LD number means a closer pass to Earth. It does **not** mean the asteroid travels along a straight line toward the Moon, meets the Moon, or will hit Earth. The label describes how far it is from Earth at its *closest approach*. It does not tell you the asteroid's width: a small object can pass farther away than a large one.

## Try the comparison together

Draw the 20 cm line and place three paper dots at 5, 10 and 20 cm. Ask, “Which dot is closest to Earth?” Then point to the Moon mark and ask, “Does the dot at 5 cm have to be beside the real Moon?” No: the marks compare **amounts of distance**, not directions or real positions in space. This simple ruler also makes clear why a picture on a phone cannot show the whole sky at one true scale.
