An **asteroid** is a mostly rocky body that orbits the Sun. A **comet** is an icy body that also orbits the Sun; near the Sun it can develop a fuzzy cloud and a tail. A **meteor** is the brief streak of light made when a small piece of space material enters an atmosphere. The simplest clue is to ask: are we naming a body in space, or the light we see in the sky?

## Three names, three clues

- **Asteroid — rock in orbit.** Asteroids are often rocky or metallic. Many travel in the broad belt between Mars and Jupiter, while others follow different orbits. They are not all headed toward Earth.
- **Comet — ice and dust in orbit.** A comet has a frozen centre mixed with dust and rock. As it approaches the Sun, warming can release gas and dust, creating a cloud around it and sometimes a tail. A comet does not have a bright tail at every point in its journey.
- **Meteor — a flash in an atmosphere.** A smaller object in space is a **meteoroid**. If it enters Earth's atmosphere and makes a streak of light, that event is a meteor. It is not a star falling from the sky.

If some material survives the journey through the air and reaches the ground, that piece is called a **meteorite**. These words describe different stages, not four names for the same thing at the same moment.

## Why can a comet and a meteor both have a “tail”?

They may look similar in a drawing, but the setting is different. A comet's dust and gas can be pushed away from the Sun while the comet is still in space. A meteor's bright streak happens within an atmosphere and lasts briefly. Small pieces shed by comets *or* asteroids can become meteoroids; some comet debris later produces meteor showers when Earth crosses its path. Not every meteor comes from a comet.

Try a family sorting game: “Rock orbiting the Sun?” Asteroid. “Icy traveller with a cloud near the Sun?” Comet. “Quick streak in our sky?” Meteor. Then add the question, “What was the small object called before it entered the air?” Meteoroid.
