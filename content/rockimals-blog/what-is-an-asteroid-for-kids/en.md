An **asteroid** is a natural object, usually made of rock and sometimes metal, that travels around the Sun. It is much smaller than a planet. Many asteroids orbit in the broad region between Mars and Jupiter, but they do not all stay there. That is the short answer to “What is an asteroid?”

## Where do asteroids come from?

Asteroids are leftovers from the early Solar System. The planets formed long ago, but not every piece of material became part of a planet. Many of these smaller pieces still travel around the Sun today.

The main **asteroid belt** lies between Mars and Jupiter. Imagine a very wide stretch of space, not a tightly packed wall of rocks. Other asteroids follow different paths. Some have orbits that bring them into Earth's part of the Solar System; scientists call those **near-Earth asteroids**.

“Near-Earth” describes an orbit. It does **not** mean that an asteroid is about to hit Earth. A predicted close pass and an impact are different things.

## Are all asteroids the same size?

No. Some are only a few metres across; others are hundreds of kilometres across. Many are irregularly shaped rather than round like the planets in a picture book. Their material also varies: rock is common, and some contain metal.

When someone says an asteroid is “small” or “large,” ask what they are measuring. Its **size** is how wide the object is. Its **approach distance** is how far it passes from Earth. A small object can pass far away, and a large one can be far away too. The animal characters in Rockimals are a playful comparison, not a photograph or a life-size model of an asteroid.

## A calm way to picture a close pass

Here is an imaginary example, not today's sky data: suppose a visitor is predicted to pass at **twice the distance between Earth and the Moon**. That number describes the gap between the visitor and Earth. It says nothing by itself about how wide the visitor is, and “close” in space does not automatically mean dangerous.

Try two questions with a child: “How big is the rock?” and “How far away will it be?” Keeping the answers separate makes a space headline easier to understand.

Rockimals uses real near-Earth object information for a visitor's official identity and selected measurements, such as size, approach distance and speed.
